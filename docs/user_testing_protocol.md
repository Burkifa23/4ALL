# Testing with real people — what to run and what to record

The report currently says two different things about human validation, and it is worth
separating them because one is achievable this week and one is not.

| Claim in the report | What it needs | Status |
|---|---|---|
| "Human raters are the first ground truth that doesn't come from the simulator" | Team members rating decisions blind | **Designed, tooled, never run** |
| "not validated against real learners" | Real students using the app | Needs participants and consent |

Neither is done. The first is a half-day of work. The second is a genuine user study.

---

## Before anything: two capture gaps

If you run a session today, **the data you most want will not be saved.**

`ui/history.add_attempt()` records only:

```
question_id · result · tests_passed · tests_total · efficiency_score · style_score
```

So the following are **shown to the student and then lost**:

- **Hint text.** `get_hint()` returns an `LLMHint`, it renders, and it is never written to
  disk. A hint-quality study run today would produce no hints to rate.
- **`big_o_time` and `raw_feedback`.** The evaluator's complexity judgement and its
  one-sentence feedback are dropped; only the two numeric scores survive. You cannot check
  afterwards whether the model said `O(N)` or `O(N^2)`.

**Fix before running anything.** Add those three fields to the `attempt` dict in
`add_attempt()` and pass the hint through from `app.py`. It is a small change and it is the
difference between a study and an anecdote. I can make it if you want it.

Everything else is already captured:

| File | Contents |
|---|---|
| `data/sessions/<id>.json` | session id, timestamps, provider/model/base_url, questions served, full attempt history |
| `data/predictions.jsonl` | every recommender call: all nine features, decision, confidence, next question |
| `data/generated/gen_*.json` | every generated question, with its computed test cases |
| `data/reports.jsonl` | questions a student reported as broken |

Note what is deliberately *not* recorded: no names, no API keys. Keep it that way.

## Study A — Human agreement on recommender decisions

**The question it answers:** does the classifier agree with human judgement more often than a
simple rule does? This is the study that converts §5.3's tautological accuracy into a claim
about pedagogy, which is exactly what the report says is missing.

**Everything for this already exists.** `docs/evaluation_plan_recommender.md` specifies the
protocol; `recommender/evaluate.py` implements `sample`, `majority`, `load_ratings` and
`report`. Nobody has run it.

**The blocker is data volume.** You have **9 logged decision points**; the protocol samples
15. Each submission in the app produces one. So:

1. **Generate decisions.** Four team members × 5–6 submissions each = ~22 decision points,
   about 30 minutes total. Vary them deliberately — some passing first time, some failing
   repeatedly, some good and some brute-force solutions — or every sampled case will look the
   same and the rating exercise will be trivial.
2. **Sample.** `python -m recommender.evaluate --sample 15` writes
   `data/evaluation/rating_sheet.csv` (features, blank decision column) and `cases.json`
   (the model's hidden answers).
3. **Rate blind.** Each of the four fills a copy as `data/evaluation/ratings_<name>.csv`,
   choosing `reinforce` or `level_up`. **Nobody opens `cases.json` first**, and Person 3
   rates as a rater, not as the model's author.
4. **Report.** `python -m recommender.evaluate --report`.

**What to record.** The four rating CSVs, plus one line per rater noting anything they found
ambiguous — disagreements are the interesting part, not noise.

**What to compute:** classifier agreement with the human majority (x/15), rule-baseline
agreement (x/15), and the number of unanimous cases, which bounds what any system could
achieve. Report **percentage agreement, not Cohen's κ** — at n=15 a chance-corrected
coefficient implies precision the data cannot support.

**What it cannot show.** Four raters who wrote the labelling policy are not independent of it.
Say so in the report. It is still the first ground truth not generated by the simulator, which
is a real step up, but it is not external validation.

## Study B — Hint quality

**The question:** does the shipped hint prompt name the concept a student actually missed?

`evaluator/testing/score_hints.py` already scores the four mechanical rules — no corrected
code, under 120 words, cites the failing case, grounded in the student's identifiers — over
ten fixtures. What it deliberately cannot judge is whether the concept named is the *right*
one. That needs a human.

**Protocol.** Run `python -m evaluator.testing.score_hints --prompt v2 --out hints.json`.
For each of the ten, a rater sees the buggy submission, the failing case and the hint, but
**not** the `should name:` line, and answers one question: *does this hint point at the actual
bug?* Yes / partly / no.

**What to record:** ten judgements per rater, and for any "no", one phrase saying what the
hint pointed at instead. That second column is what makes the result useful rather than just a
score.

This costs about twenty minutes and gives the ethics and evaluation sections a number for a
component that currently has none.

## Study C — Real students using the platform

**The question:** is the system usable, and do real learners behave anything like the
simulated archetypes the recommender was trained on?

### Participants

**Five to eight** students who have taken introductory Python. Below five you learn nothing
generalisable; above eight you spend the time you do not have. They should not be teammates —
you already know how the system works, which is exactly the knowledge a usability test needs
participants to lack.

### Consent — do this properly, it is also worth marks

Before anyone touches the app, give each participant a short written statement and get a
signature or a dated email reply. It needs to say:

- what the system does, and that it uses AI models to generate feedback;
- what is recorded (code submissions, test results, scores, recommendations) and what is not
  (names, student IDs);
- where the model runs — **if you use a cloud endpoint, their code leaves the institution**,
  and they must be told before they consent, not after;
- that participation is voluntary, has no bearing on any grade, and can stop at any moment;
- that the data appears in a coursework report in aggregate, with no individual identified;
- who to contact to have their session deleted.

Assign each participant a code (P1…P8) and keep the name–code mapping **out of the
repository**. The session files already use random identifiers, so record the mapping
separately and destroy it after the report is submitted.

Your Ethics section can then describe a consent procedure you actually followed rather than
one you would recommend, which is a materially stronger paragraph.

### Session protocol (~25 minutes each)

1. **Brief (2 min).** Consent, and: "think aloud — tell me what you are trying to do and what
   confuses you. You are testing the system, it is not testing you."
2. **Baseline task (10 min).** They attempt the served question. Do not help. Let them fail;
   failures are what produce hints and reinforcement decisions, which is half your data.
3. **Custom Practice (8 min).** Ask them to request a topic of their choice, so the generator
   is exercised on inputs your team did not pick.
4. **Debrief (5 min).** Four questions, same for everyone:
   - Was the next question the system chose a reasonable one? Why?
   - Did the hint help you find your own mistake, or did it give the answer away, or neither?
   - Did you understand where the feedback came from?
   - Would you use this to practise?

### What to record

**Automatic** — nothing to do beyond keeping the files: session transcripts, prediction log,
generated questions.

**By hand, one row per participant:**

| Field | Why |
|---|---|
| Participant code | P1…P8 |
| Session id | links to `data/sessions/<id>.json` |
| Questions attempted, passes/failures | context for their answers |
| Time on first question | usability signal |
| Recommendation judged reasonable? (Y/N + reason) | Study A's question, from the learner's side |
| Hint verdict (helped / gave it away / useless) | the hint metric you currently lack |
| Any generated question served, and whether it was solvable | generator behaviour in the wild |
| Observed confusions, verbatim | the usability findings |
| Anything that broke | failures never reach the logs if the app errors |

Two people per session: one facilitates, one writes. Trying to do both means losing the
verbatim quotes, which are the most quotable evidence you will get.

### What to compute

- Recommendation agreement: participants judging the next question reasonable, x/n.
- Hint usefulness: counts across the three categories.
- Generator success in the wild: generated questions served ÷ requested.
- Real-vs-simulated comparison: pass rate and attempts-per-question from real sessions
  against the simulator's archetype parameters. **This is the highest-value number in the
  whole study**, because it is the first evidence for or against the assumption every
  recommender figure rests on.
- Usability issues, grouped by frequency.

### What it cannot show

With five to eight participants and one session each, you cannot claim learning gains,
statistical significance, or generalisation beyond your cohort. Claim what it does support:
that the system is usable, that its recommendations are or are not judged reasonable, and
whether real behaviour resembles the simulator. Overclaiming here would undo the credibility
the rest of the report earns by hedging carefully.

## What to write, and where

| Study | Section | Roughly |
|---|---|---|
| A | §5.3, after the qualification paragraph | 4–5 sentences with the agreement figures |
| B | §5.2 or §6 | 2–3 sentences |
| C | new §5.5 "User evaluation" | one paragraph of method, one of findings |
| Consent procedure | §6 Ethical Considerations, Privacy | 2 sentences |

Then amend §7: "not validated against real learners" becomes "validated against *n*
participants in a single session each, which supports usability claims but not learning
claims."

**If you only have time for one, run Study A.** It is already designed and tooled, it needs no
external participants or consent, it takes half a day, and it targets the single weakest claim
in the report — that the recommender's accuracy measures its own labelling policy rather than
anything pedagogical.
